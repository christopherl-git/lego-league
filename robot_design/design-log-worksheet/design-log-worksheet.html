This is our team's record of how we built and changed our robot. At judging, the judges will ask us why we made each choice and what we changed. Some of the answers are written down. Some are missing, and only the people who did the work know them. That is where you come in.

What to do

  1. Read each decision on pages 3 to 12. The gold box shows what we already have written down.
  2. Fix it if something is wrong. Cross it out and write the right answer beside it.
  3. Fill in every ✎ question you know the answer to. Put your name next to the ones you worked on.
  4. Add anything we forgot on pages 14 and 15. Write the test numbers on page 13.
  5. Hand it back to Chris when you are done. Page 2 is a checklist to tick off.

What makes a good answer?

  • Say why. "We changed it because ..." beats "We changed it."
  • Use numbers. "It drove 100 cm and ended 1 cm off" is better than "it was pretty good."
  • Say what did not work. Judges like honest mistakes. It shows we kept improving.
  • Not sure? Write what you remember and put a "?" next to it. A guess we can check is better than a blank.

The robot design process we follow

Identify the problem → Design a fix → Create it → Iterate (test and improve) → Communicate what we learned. This log is the proof of that.

1We chose HummerOne as our base robotDecided 2026-09-22
What our log says We shortlisted four designs: HummerOne, Comp Bot, StarterBot, and GummyBears / Droid Bot M. We picked HummerOne because it is free, has a full-width attachment bar (swaps in about 5 seconds, versus about 20 for a pinned design), and its frame should square well against a wall. It has 73 build steps. We chose not to buy StarterBot ($30). The build was finished on 2026-10-01.

3Front wheels: mine cart wheelsBefore 2026-10-01
What our log says HummerOne's front wheels have no rubber, so they skid unevenly from run to run. We swapped in mine cart wheels, which are lower and create less friction when turning. The conference taught us that smaller wheels wobble less and that accuracy matters more than speed. Problem: the new wheels still stuck out past the sides, so a wheel could touch a wall before the body. They were good in a straight line but resisted turning. We replaced them in entry 9.

4We added a second colour sensor2026-09-27 to 09-29, Jack
What our log says At the conference we learned that squaring up on a line needs two colour sensors: when one sees black, the robot turns until the other sees black too, then wiggles straight. In the gym we could not square up with one sensor, so Jack added a second. The square-up program was finished on 2026-10-01. Still to do: find which lines in front of mission models we can square up on, and show a judge a before and after.

7Attachment pins: axle pins tried, quick pins keptTested 2026-10-01
What our log says Jack suggested axle pins as an alternative to quick pins. While testing the lifting arm with the seed bucket, the attachment kept coming off with axle pins. We went back to quick pins, which hold more securely. A secure fit matters more than swap speed, because an attachment that falls off mid-run loses points and time.

8Gyro PID drive and turn blocks (Mission 2 code)2026-10-06, Jordan and Fedor
What our log says Yaw (gyro) turns the robot by a real angle, but 90 motor degrees only turns a wheel a quarter turn. Gyro Move drives straight by fixing its heading error every 0.1 seconds (Kp 2.1, Ki 2), with a speed ramp. Gyro Turn slows as it nears the angle and stops 3 degrees early so it does not overshoot. Both are reusable My Blocks. Used in a Mission 2 test run on 2026-10-06.
